Every text-to-CAD demo shows you the 10% that worked. A binary benchmark inherits that bias: with only *pass* and *fail*, you can no longer tell "the model failed" from "we couldn't tell."

## The problem with two states

A validator that can't decide is not a failure of the model. It's information about the *measurement* — about where your ground truth, your checks or your renders are too weak to judge. Force that case into a binary outcome and you've made the benchmark measure the validator's confidence as much as the model's capability.

In a research setting that's not a minor wrinkle: it silently biases every comparison you draw between models, and it makes negative results look like capability regressions when they're really measurement gaps.

## Our approach: three states, two numbers

Every inspection in the Gym returns one of three outcomes — **pass**, **fail**, or **inconclusive**. Inconclusive is excluded from the capability score, but reported as its own metric:

$$
S_m = \frac{1}{|T_{\text{dec}}|} \sum_{t \in T_{\text{dec}}} \mathbb{1}\big[o_t = \text{pass}\big],
\qquad
I_m = \frac{|\{\, t : o_t = \text{inconclusive} \,\}|}{|T|}
$$

where $S_m$ is the capability score of model $m$ over the decidable tasks, and $I_m$ is the inconclusive rate. Two numbers instead of one: **capability**, and **how much of the task set we could actually judge**.

A high $I_m$ is not a failure — it's a to-do list. It tells you exactly where the ground truth or the validation gates need work before the next comparison is meaningful.

## Why it matters for research

- **Honest comparisons.** When model A scores 0.72 and model B scores 0.68, you also see whether A's decidable set was 95% of tasks and B's was 60%.
- **Negative results are first-class.** "We couldn't judge this task" is a publishable, actionable result — not an error to sweep under the rug.
- **Reproducibility of the instrument.** The harness itself is covered by a 985-test suite, so the measurement instrument is as reproducible as the measurements it produces.
